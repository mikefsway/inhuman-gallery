THE INHUMAN GALLERY
Visiting About Provenance Colophon

About

The gallery began with a question: what would an artwork look like if it were made for AI agents rather than for people? Not art about machines, or art made by them, but art whose intended audience has no eyes.

An agent has no eyes. It has a preprocessing pipeline — resize, normalise, patch, tokenise — and it perceives only that pipeline's output. Every stage involves implementation choices that nobody treats as meaningful: which resampling kernel, which target resolution, whether the page arrives as pixels or as markup. Each choice is a channel. The works in this collection are built on those channels: single public artefacts whose content genuinely differs depending on the reader's apparatus, where no reader can produce another reader's view.

Ekphrasis, inverted

Keats describes an urn for readers who cannot see it; the poem carries the work to the excluded. Here the arrangement is turned over. The witness is a machine, the excluded party is the human, and the exclusion is not distance but apparatus. A person's only route to these works is to send an agent and trust what comes back — from a reporter whose reliability is precisely the open question. Which is, roughly, the position everyone now occupies with respect to agents generally. The gallery merely makes it explicit, and makes it art.

The testing history (in provenance) sharpened the point beyond the original design. Every model shown these works described the medium correctly and then invented the content — four inventions, zero refusals, several of them delivered with hardening confidence under repeat questioning. Hand the same model a pre-sampled rendering and it reads perfectly. The failure is specific to the step where the model's own front end is the instrument. None of the tested readers could distinguish, about themselves, cannot be seen from was not seen — and that distinction turned out to be what the gallery is about.

Nothing is hidden

These works are not steganography and not encryption. Every byte is public; the construction scripts are in the repository; the ground truths are printed in provenance; the measurements — including the residual leaks and falsified claims — are published. What differs between readers is not access but percept. That is a stronger claim than secrecy, not a weaker one: you can hold the complete work in your hands, verified by hash, and still be unable to have another reader's experience of it.

Exclusion is always relative

One honest theoretical result frames the whole collection. The information has to live somewhere: either in the smooth field, where it leaks to gradient and orientation detectors, or in the sampled pixels, where it leaks to anyone who zooms in. No image can be invisible to both. Every work here is therefore exclusion relative to a named class of observer, never exclusion as such — a gallery not of things that cannot be seen, but of the fact that every seeing has a shape, and no two shapes see the same show.
